    1. The Parent & Student Readiness Playbook (3 Phases)

    Whether your student is just discovering problem-solving or aiming for national Olympiads, success follows a clear three-phase annual cycle:

    Phase 1: Fall (Sept – Nov)

    Secure Testing Sites Early

    Confirm whether your child's school hosts MOEMS, AMC 8, and MATHCOUNTS. If not, partner with a teacher to register the school before deadlines or reserve a seat at an accredited external test center (such as an AoPS Academy or local university).

    Phase 2: Fall – Winter

    Build Problem-Solving Habits

    Move beyond school speed-drills. Dedicate 3–4 hours per week to creative problem-solving (Prealgebra, Counting, Number Theory, Geometry) using free past exam archives and weekly peer study sessions.

    Phase 3: Jan – May

    Compete, Review & Level Up

    Sit for AMC 8 (Jan), MATHCOUNTS (Feb), and Math Kangaroo (Mar). Pick up contest booklets afterward to re-solve missed problems—turning every exam into a stepping stone toward AMC 10 and AIME.

    2. How to Have Your Child's School Become an Official Test Site

    Many elementary and middle schools do not automatically administer MAA (AMC) or MOEMS exams simply because no parent or faculty member has championed them yet. Important MAA Rule: Parents cannot register a school directly—a school employee (Math Teacher, Department Chair, Gifted Coordinator, or Principal) must serve as the official Competition Manager.

    4 Steps for Parents to Help Their School Host:

    • Step 1 — Approach a Faculty Champion: Reach out to your child's math teacher, gifted/enrichment specialist, or principal early in the fall (or at least 3 weeks before the Jan 5 AMC 8 deadline).
    • Step 2 — Keep the Ask Simple: Explain that administering the AMC 8 or MOEMS takes only a single 45–60 minute after-school or lunch block, and offer to coordinate parent volunteers to help proctor or cover the modest registration fee via the PTA/PTO if needed.
    • Step 3 — Official Registration Portal: Direct the school's Competition Manager to register online at the MAA AMC Registration Portal (for AMC 8/10/12), MOEMS.org (for Grades 4–8 monthly rounds), or MATHCOUNTS.org (for Grades 6–8).
    • Step 4 — External Testing Center Backup: If your school cannot host this year, use the MAA AMC Locations Directory or register individually for Math Kangaroo (by Dec 31) at a nearby university, math circle, or AoPS Academy testing center.

    4. Step-by-Step Pathway: 6th Grade to MIT, Stanford, Princeton & Caltech

    Stage 1: Middle School Foundation (Grades 6–7) Target Math: Prealgebra → Algebra 1, Counting & Number Theory
    Competition Milestones
    • Compete in all 5 rounds of MOEMS (Nov–Mar) to build comfort with open-ended answers.
    • Sit for AMC 8 (Jan) and Math Kangaroo (Mar); aim for AMC 8 Honor Roll (Top 5%) or Distinguished Honor Roll (Top 1%).
    • Try out for the school MATHCOUNTS team.
    Preparation & Balance Habits
    • Use AoPS Alcumus (free) and AoPS textbooks to build conceptual depth rather than rote memorization.
    • Protect creative & physical passions (music, dance, athletics)—top universities value sustained depth outside math.
    Stage 2: High School Transition & Proof Writing (Grades 7–9) Target Math: Geometry, Intermediate Algebra & Intro Proofs
    Competition Milestones
    • Take AMC 10 (Nov) once Algebra 1 & Geometry are mastered; top ~2.5%–5% qualify for the invite-only AIME.
    • Participate in USAMTS (month-long proof-based rounds) to learn formal mathematical proof writing.
    • Advance through MATHCOUNTS Chapter and State rounds.
    Strategic Focus
    • Qualifying for AIME before 10th grade is a major milestone showing readiness for Olympiad-level reasoning.
    • Always review missed contest solutions—true growth happens when analyzing problems you couldn't solve under time pressure.
    Stage 3: National Invitational Tier (Grades 9–11) Target Math: Precalculus, Trigonometry & Olympiad Proofs
    Competition Milestones
    • Take AMC 12 (Nov) and excel on the AIME (15 integer-answer questions in 3 hours; aim for 7+ out of 15).
    • Qualify for USAJMO / USAMO (top ~250–500 students nationwide; 2-day, 9-hour proof exam).
    • Compete in collegiate invitationals: HMMT (Harvard-MIT), PUMaC (Princeton), SMT (Stanford), BMT (Berkeley).
    Leadership & Community
    • Captain the high school math team or mentor younger middle school students in a local Math Circle.
    • Apply to selective proof-based summer programs (PROMYS, Ross Mathematics Program, Canada/USA Mathcamp).
    Stage 4: Olympiad Camp & University Admissions (Grades 11–12) Target: MOP / IMO & Top STEM Admissions
    Peak Achievements
    • Invitation to MOP (Mathematical Olympiad Program, top ~60 USAMO/USAJMO scorers) → Team USA selection for the IMO.
    • Selective summer research programs (RSI, MIT PRIMES).
    College Application Impact
    • MIT, Caltech, Princeton, and Stanford specifically ask for AMC and AIME scores on their applications.
    • Strong AMC/AIME/USAMO results paired with authentic non-math pursuits (arts, athletics, community leadership) create an unforgettable admissions profile.

    5. How Parents & Kids Can Build a Local Math Circle Together

    Problem-solving thrives in a supportive peer community. Any group of parents can start a thriving neighborhood or school Math Circle in four simple steps:

    • 1. Gather 4–8 Curious Students: Connect with families from your child's school, PTA, or extracurricular activities who enjoy puzzles and logic games.
    • 2. Use Free Open-Source Problem Sets: Pull weekly sets from the AoPS Wiki, MATHCOUNTS Past Competitions, or National Association of Math Circles.
    • 3. Focus on Student Explanations, Not Lectures: Rotate hosting at a local library study room, school classroom, or living room. Parents act as facilitators while kids take turns at a whiteboard explaining how they arrived at an answer—celebrating multiple solution paths.
    • 4. Run Monthly Mock Exams: Once a month, proctor a real timed 40-minute AMC 8 or 30-minute MOEMS round together, followed by snacks and a collaborative group review of the toughest 5 problems.
